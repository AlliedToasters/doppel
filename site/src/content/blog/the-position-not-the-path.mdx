Chess players talk about "being in book": still in the opening lines everyone knows. To a bot that imitates
people, "book" is a question about counting. How many people have stood in this exact position before? We
counted every position in 22.7 million Lichess games from October 2026, about a billion different positions.
Then we asked what that count tells us about how people play.

## Same position, different route

There are two ways to say "this position has been seen before":

- **By move order.** The game is still following a sequence of moves that other games followed.
- **By position.** The pieces are on squares where they have stood before, whatever the order of moves.

The two differ when games **transpose**. 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 and 1.Nf3 Nc6 2.e4 e5 reach the same board. By move
order they are two different lines; by position they are one.

So a position reached by an unusual order can still be well known. Counting by position keeps a typical 1500-rated
game "in book" three plies longer: 15 instead of 12. At 2400 the gap is five plies.

From 6.7% of moves in unseen positions down to 0.5% in the most familiar ones, a thirteen-fold difference.
After controlling for move number, rating and clock, the position count explains about **twice as much** of
the variation in think time as the move-order count, and **six times as much** of the variation in blunders.

The clearest evidence is in games that transpose. When such a game leaves the known *move order*, think
time bumps briefly and settles back. When it later reaches an unknown *position*, think time jumps by about 40% and stays high.
People react to the board, not to the route.

## Your openings give you away

Counting positions works for a single player too. We built a personal book for each of 8,000 players from their
earlier games, then tried to pick each player out of a pool of 1,000 from 40 new games.

Which positions you choose to reach, weighted towards the rare ones, finds you 80% of the time from 40 games.
General style statistics manage 33%. And where you've been before, you repeat yourself: your own earlier move
predicts your next one 82% of the time, while the most popular move among players at your level gets 47%.
The same holds at scale. Across 881k players in a month of games, a player's own earlier move in a position is
right 83% of the time; the crowd's most common move gets 50%. Nearly all of it is in the first ten plies: that is
where people return to positions they have played before.

## What this means for Doppel

1. **Doppel's sense of "book" should count positions, not move orders.** That's what people respond to.
2. **Your repertoire is the target, not the policy.** Where you've been before, your own earlier move is the
   yardstick our models are scored against. Simply replaying it would copy your openings and model nothing
   else.
3. **Expect hesitation and errors where they belong.** A bot that thinks for the same time in a well-known
   position as in a strange one doesn't feel human. The count of the position is a cheap, strong signal for
   when to slow down. The last few moves of the current game tell how much. Think time and the size of errors
   carry over within a game; move choice doesn't. The current game explains 9.1% of the variation in think time,
   more than the player does (8.1%), and 3.4% in error size, but about none in move choice. Once we know the player, this game's earlier moves say nothing
   more about which move comes next.
