Most chess bots are engines with the strength turned down. They play near-perfect moves, then throw in a random
blunder for you to exploit. Anyone who has played one knows the feeling: it doesn't play like a person. It never
panics with ten seconds left. It never misses the knight fork that a real 1200 would miss, and it never finds the one
a real 1200 would find. It moves instantly in positions where a person would stop and think.

Doppel is an attempt to build another kind of synthetic opponent: one that plays like a real person at your level.

## Why Doppel

Doppel started from three frustrations and one goal: to be good company across a chessboard.

1. **Synthetic opponents don't feel human.** Even the best human-imitation models play every game like an average
   player at their rating. Real people have sharp days and sloppy days.
2. **Human opponents come with social friction.** Stepping away for a minute in a timed game feels rude. A bot
   doesn't mind if you pause.
3. **Practice against bots doesn't transfer.** Hours against human-like bots didn't prepare me for real people on
   Lichess. In robotics this is called the sim-to-real gap: what works in simulation fails in the world.

### The missing rung

As a kid in chess club I never got traction: no early wins, so no interest, so no progress. As an adult, lessons
in the style of Duolingo got me past that, but nothing prepared me for live human play. Doppel is meant to be the
rung between lessons and playing people:

- **Opponents tuned so you win sometimes**, about 40 to 50% of the time.
- **Losses that feel beatable**, because a Doppel having a bad game blunders like a person, not like random noise.
- **Honest progress.** A Doppel's weights are frozen, so its average strength never changes. If you start beating
  it more often, you improved.

The ladder goes: Doppels to practice, Lichess to test it against real people, and social enjoyment of over-the-board games as the goal.

## The shoulders we stand on

In 1948 Alan Turing and David Champernowne wrote a chess program before any computer could run it; Turing played
it by working through the algorithm by hand, taking about half an hour a move. In 1950 Claude Shannon wrote down how
a computer should search a chess game, and engines followed that plan for decades. In 1997 Deep Blue beat Garry
Kasparov. Today Stockfish, free and open source, plays far beyond any human, and AlphaZero and the open-source
Leela Chess Zero showed that a network could teach itself the game from nothing but the rules. These machines
changed how people study chess, and every serious player now learns with one at their side.

None of them plays like a person, and none was meant to. The work closest to ours is **Maia**, from Ashton
Anderson, Jon Kleinberg, Siddhartha Sen, Reid McIlroy-Young and colleagues. Their 2020 paper put "aligning
superhuman AI with human behavior" in its title at a time when that was an unusual thing to want, and it set the
goal as faithfulness to what people actually do, rather than to what is correct. Three of its points shaped Doppel:

- **Weakening an engine doesn't make it human.** A depth-limited Stockfish doesn't make a 1200's mistakes; it makes
  its own. Maia solved this for skill by training on millions of human games at each rating.
- **Clock and context drive errors.** Their blunder predictor got noticeably better just from knowing the players'
  ratings and the time left on the clock.
- **The future work they named:** telling apart players with the same rating. That is exactly what Doppel is about.

They went on to model individual players, to fold every rating into one model, and to think hard about the ethics of
it: their 2022 paper *Mimetic Models: Ethical Implications of AI that Acts Like You* anticipated many of the questions
a project like ours raises. Their work is why we could start where we did: the Doppel you can play today chooses its
moves with Maia-3. We also owe a lot to **Allie**, from Yiming Zhang, Daphne Ippolito and colleagues at Carnegie
Mellon, which learned human think times and when people resign.

### Experiment zero

Before training anything, we measured whether existing bots reproduce how people vary from game to game. They
don't lack variety: their games are as spread out in quality as people's are. What they lack is *persistence*.
In human games, how well someone played the first half predicts how well they play the second. In Maia-3's games
it doesn't. People carry a state through a game: focus, fatigue, frustration. Bots don't. That gap is the first
thing Doppel is built to fill, and we'll write the experiment up properly in a later post.

## Beyond Maia: a population of players

Maia answers "what would a 1500 play here?" But there is no such thing as *a* 1500. One 1500 plays the King's
Gambit every game and loves a messy position. Another trades queens at the first chance and grinds out endgames.
One plays fast and loose; another burns half their clock on move twelve. They have the same rating and play
nothing alike.

We want to build that population: many distinct players at every level, each with their own openings, habits,
clock and temperament, and moods that come and go from game to game. A Doppel should be:

1. **Human in its moves.** The moves a player like this actually plays, learned from millions of real games.
2. **Human in its mistakes.** Errors where people make them: in strange positions, under time pressure, late in a
   long game. Not at random.
3. **Human in its time.** Quick in familiar positions, slow when it's lost the thread, scrappy when its clock runs low.
4. **Someone in particular.** A persistent player with a style you can get to know, and an off day now and then.
5. **You, if you want it.** If you choose to share your own games, a Doppel that plays like *you*: your openings,
   your habits, your favourite ways of going wrong.

Why bother? Because playing a person teaches you things an engine can't. A human-like opponent at your level is
a fair game you can actually learn from. A double of yourself is a mirror: it shows you the habits you can't
see from the inside. And building one means answering a question that's interesting on its own: what makes
someone's chess *theirs*?

## Use Cases

These are possibilities, not promises, but they're the ones that excite us most.

### Coaching by simulation

A human coach judges your weaknesses from a handful of your games: a small, noisy sample. A Doppel of you could
play thousands of games against a varied field of opponents and come back with the one weakness that costs you the
most.

- **Rank weaknesses by what they cost, not how often they appear.** Fix one weakness in your Doppel (make it play
  knight endgames correctly, say), rerun the games, and measure how many points it gains. The fix that gains the
  most is the one to train.
- **Check against reality.** Whatever the simulation finds should also show up in your real games that the Doppel
  never saw. If it doesn't, the flaw may be in the model, not in you.
- **Close the loop.** Positions where your Doppel goes wrong become your personal puzzle set. Then check whether the
  weakness actually fades in your real games, and refit your Doppel every few weeks as you improve.

Simulations produce positions and statistics, not lessons. A language model could turn them into advice a person
can take in. But language models are still weak at chess, so it never calculates: every claim it makes is checked
against the data before it reaches you.

### Preparing for an opponent

Fair-play rules ban help during a game, not before it. Strong players already prepare openings twenty moves deep
against a specific opponent. A Doppel extends that from "what will they play?" to "how do they play when they're
short of time, worse, or rattled?" Top players have teams of helpers for this; free, open Doppels could give the
same to everyone else.

It gets interesting when both sides do it. If I know you've rehearsed against my Doppel, my best move is to play
against my own habits, and you know that too. Does being modeled change how people play? That's a question we can
measure.

### The masters of the past

If a few dozen games are enough to capture how someone plays, the person on the other side of the board doesn't
have to be alive. Paul Morphy, José Raúl Capablanca, Vera Menchik: hundreds of their games survive. What would it
feel like to sit across from them? Or to watch Morphy play Mikhail Tal, a century apart?

The hard parts are real. Playing at their strength needs search, not just imitation. A model trained on modern
games smuggles in modern opening knowledge that Morphy never had. The rules themselves shifted for centuries:
castling and promotion varied by country, chess clocks only arrived in the mid-1800s, and nobody recorded how long
each move took until the digital era. And history keeps the famous wins more often than the quiet losses. A match
between frozen champion Doppels would be a new kind of evidence in chess's oldest argument, who was the greatest,
with the honest caveat that it compares models, not people.

### Famous people who happened to play

The artist Marcel Duchamp played for France at the Chess Olympiad. The composer Sergei Prokofiev beat Capablanca in
a simultaneous exhibition. Humphrey Bogart was a strong amateur who once hustled games for small change in New
York parks, and Leo Tolstoy played all his life. Some of their games were recorded.

### Play against Napoleon?

A handful of games are attributed to him. Historians doubt the brilliant ones; the games that were actually
witnessed look like a beginner's. Games attributed to Lenin and Stalin are widely considered fakes. Which raises a
fun idea: a model that knows how a player plays can also say how likely it is that they played a given game. The
same machinery that builds a Doppel could help sort real historical games from flattering fakes.

### Whose Doppel?

All of this raises a question we take seriously: who gets to be imitated? Our rule is simple. Doppel will never
publish an imitation of a living player without their express consent. Players who are no longer alive need a
rule of their own, and we'd like to engage thoughtfully with the community about how to proceed building this.

## Beyond chess

Chess is a small world with something most of AI research lacks: ground truth. We know who played every move,
how strong they were, and, thanks to engines, exactly how good each move was. That makes it a place to test ideas
cheaply that matter far outside the game.

- **Personas in language models.** Chatbots suffer the same collapse as chess bots: one averaged personality and no
  state that persists. Researchers have found that traits like these show up as directions inside a model. In
  chess we can check the same ideas against the truth: is there a "tilt" direction inside a move model, and when
  does a model work out who it's imitating?
- **Superhuman AI and humans at every level.** An engine that knows your Doppel could choose moves that are
  objectively a little worse but most likely to make *you* go wrong. That is a measurable version of an AI
  optimizing against a model of human weakness instead of toward the truth. How much does it gain? Do people
  notice? Can a monitor catch it? Or flip it around: give a player a superhuman advisor that is sometimes subtly
  wrong, and see whether they can tell.
- **Does practice with an AI make you better with people?** The worry about AI companions isn't a bad one; it's a
  good one that people prefer to each other. Chess makes that measurable: does time with Doppels raise your rating
  against humans, and do you keep choosing to play humans?

A chess move can't persuade anyone of anything, so chess is a safe place to push human-likeness as far as it goes,
in the open. Where the stakes are higher, the same methods are just as useful for detection as for imitation.

## An experiment on myself

Can an adult become an unusually fast-improving player with this kind of coaching? I intend to find out, with the
rules written down before I start:

- **A baseline:** my current Lichess rating and how it has been moving.
- **A comparison group:** players from the open Lichess database who started where I am, with similar volume and
  account age. Their rating curves show what would have happened anyway.
- **A goal set in advance:** for example, finishing above the 90th percentile of that group's six-month gain.
- **Over-the-board tournaments** at the start and the end, so a fast online rise can't be mistaken for cheating.
- **A public log here**, success or not.

One person proves only that it can happen, not how often. But it's the pilot for offering the same comparison to
every player who wants it.

## Why the research is open

We don't know yet how to build most of this. So we're doing the science in public:

- **The code is open** (AGPL-3.0), and so are the results, in our [repository](https://github.com/AlliedToasters/doppel).
- **Every claim gets a yardstick.** Each thing we model is scored against a simple baseline, against other chess
  bots, and, where we can measure it, against how consistent people are with themselves. A clever model that
  can't beat a simple rule hasn't learned anything.
- **Failed results count.** Our first model of "who a player is" failed its own tests. We'll write about that too.
- **Public models use freely licensed games only.** Lichess publishes its games openly, and that is what our released
  models learn from.
- **Free to play, and your data is not for sale.** If we ever charge for anything, it will be optional extras, never
  the game itself, and never your data.

## What these lab notes are

This is where we'll write up what we learn as we go, with the data behind each chart. Some posts will be about
