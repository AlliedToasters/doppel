import r from '../../results/allie_v0e_v0g_b.json';

export const ks = [0, 1, 5, 10, 40];
export const gain = ks.map((k) => 100 * (r.by_k[String(k)].top1_doppel - r.joined.maia3_top1py));

Models that predict human chess moves are usually scored on one number: how often their top choice is the move
the person actually played. On the standard test set, about 884,000 positions from Lichess blitz games in 2022
(first used for the Allie model), the best recent models all land within about a point of each other: 56 to 57%.
Maia-3 gets 57.1%.

That looks like a wall. Our bet is that part of it is a wall only for models that don't know *who* is playing.

## Why a general model stalls

When a player reaches a position they've played before, they repeat their earlier move about 77% of the time.
On the same positions Maia-3 gets 61%. A model that treats every 1500 player as the same person can't use that.

The benchmark makes this hard to see: it starts scoring at move 10, after the stretch where people repeat
themselves most. Over a whole game, that cut removes about 95% of what exact repeats are worth.

## The test

We took the test games where both players' ratings average 1400 to 1600 (2,021 games, 97,306 scored positions,
about 11% of the benchmark). For each player we pulled up to 40 of their *earlier* 2022 games from the Lichess
archive: strictly before the test game, never a test game.

The Doppel reads those games and condenses them into a short description of the player. A small add-on uses it
to adjust Maia-3's move probabilities. Maia-3 itself is frozen. The model was trained on 2026 games and applied to
2022 unchanged, so it has never seen these players.

With no earlier games, the Doppel is Maia-3 to within noise. With 40, it gains 0.18 points (95% interval 0.08 to
0.29). Adding a lookup on top ("if you've played this exact position before, predict your most common earlier
move") brings the gain to **0.26 points** (0.16 to 0.37).

The lookup alone, added to Maia-3, gains 0.10. So the player description adds 0.16 points (0.06 to 0.26) beyond
exact repeats.

## How big is that?

Small. A quarter of a point is about 250 more correct moves per 100,000. But the differences between the best
published models on this test are about a point, and this gain comes from the player alone: same base model,
same positions.

Three caveats:

1. **One rating bucket.** Models for other ratings are training now.
2. **The intervals are wide.** They come from about 2,000 games; the whole test set would make them about three
   times tighter.
3. **The add-on is deliberately weak.** It can only nudge Maia-3's final answer, not change how Maia-3 reads the
   board.

## What's next

The third caveat is the interesting one. The gain is concentrated in moves 10 to 19, where people play their
repertoire. Later in the game, a nudge to the final answer does little.

Next we'll try retrieval: the model looks up the positions from your earlier games that most resemble the one in
front of you, and what you played there. After that, we'll put the player description inside the model, where it
can change how a position is read, not only which move wins.
