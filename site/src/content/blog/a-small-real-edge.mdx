Models that predict human chess moves are usually scored on one number: how often their top choice is the move
the person actually played. On the standard test set, about 884,000 positions from Lichess blitz games in 2022
(first used for the Allie model), the best recent models all land within about a point of each other: 56 to 57%.
Maia-3 gets 57.1%.

That looks like a wall. Our bet is that part of it is a wall only for models that don't know *who* is playing.

## Why a general model stalls

When a player reaches a position they've played before, they repeat their earlier move about 77% of the time.
On the same positions Maia-3 gets 62%. A model that treats every 1500 player as the same person can't use that.

The benchmark makes this hard to see: it starts scoring at move 10, after the stretch where people repeat
themselves most. Over a whole game, that cut removes about 95% of what exact repeats are worth.

## The test

We took the test games where both players' ratings average 1400 to 1600 (2,021 games, 97,306 scored positions,
about 11% of the benchmark). For each player we pulled up to 40 of their *earlier* 2022 games from the Lichess
archive: strictly before the test game, never a test game.

The Doppel reads those games and condenses them into a short description of the player. A small add-on uses it
to adjust Maia-3's move probabilities. Maia-3 itself is frozen. The model was trained on 2026 games and applied to
2022 unchanged, so it has never seen these players.

## The result

<LineChart
  title="Top-1 gain over Maia-3, by earlier games read"

With no earlier games, the Doppel is Maia-3 to within noise. With 40, it gains 0.17 points (95% interval 0.07 to
0.27). Adding a lookup on top ("if you've played this exact position before, predict your most common earlier
move") brings the gain to **0.25 points** (0.15 to 0.36).

The lookup alone, added to Maia-3, gains 0.10. So the player description adds 0.16 points (0.05 to 0.26) beyond
exact repeats.

## Every rating band

We then trained the same model for two more rating bands, 1000–1400 and 1600–2000, and scored each on its own part
of the benchmark. Together the three bands cover 495,000 positions, more than half the test set.

All three clear zero. The gain is largest for the lowest-rated players and shrinks as ratings rise. The lookup
runs the other way: stronger players repeat their openings more, so at 1600–2000 the lookup adds 0.20 points, and at
1000–1400 only 0.03. Beyond exact repeats, the player description still adds 0.25, 0.16 and 0.09 points.

## Where the gain lives

Some positions have one obvious move: almost everyone plays it, whoever they are. Others leave several reasonable
choices. We sorted the 1400–1600 positions by how spread out Maia-3's predictions are, into five equal groups.

When one move is obvious, a player's history adds nothing. All of the gain is in positions where people disagree,
rising to 0.44 points (0.13 to 0.73) in the most open fifth. Knowing the player works as a tie-breaker among
plausible moves. An average over every position, the obvious ones included, makes it look smaller than it is.

## How big is that?

Small. A quarter of a point is about 250 more correct moves per 100,000. But the differences between the best
published models on this test are about a point, and this gain comes from the player alone: same base model,
same positions.

Two caveats:

1. **The intervals are still wide.** Each band rests on 2,000 to 4,000 games.
2. **The add-on is deliberately weak.** It can only nudge Maia-3's final answer, not change how Maia-3 reads the
   board.

## What's next

The second caveat is the interesting one. Our first try at retrieval, with no training at all, makes the point.
For each position, it finds the most similar positions in the player's earlier games, as Maia-3 itself sees them,
and lets those vote with what the player played there. It gains 0.11 points (0.07 to 0.15); the same lookup run on
other players' games gains nothing. But all of that gain is in moves 10 to 19, and only when a near-identical
position exists. Maia-3's own view of the board finds near-copies of a player's openings, not their style in
new positions.

So the next step is to learn the lookup, and after that to put the player description inside the model, where it
can change how a position is read, not only which move wins.
