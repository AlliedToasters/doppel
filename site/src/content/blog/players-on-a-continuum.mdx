The last post gave every player a small adapter on top of Maia-3, trained on their own earlier games. Each adapter
is a point in a space with a few hundred thousand dimensions. This post is about the shape of that space.

## Do players come in types?

If they do, a bot for a new player could start from their type. To test it we needed a distance between players. We
used what the adapters *do*: how each one changes Maia-3's move probabilities on the same 2,560 positions. Two
players are close when their adapters push the same moves up and down.

Then, for every player in the test set, we scored their test game with *another* player's adapter, picked at a range
of distances: their nearest neighbour, the next few, and so on out to the farthest player in the band.

The closer the donor, the less it costs, all the way out, with no step anywhere. To look for types directly we
grouped the players into 8 or 32 clusters and asked: at the *same* distance, is a donor from your own cluster better
than one across a border? It isn't. The difference is within noise in every band (at 8 clusters, all five within
±0.08 points, against a standard error of 0.05–0.07).

**Players don't come in types. They sit on a continuum**, and any number of clusters is a choice of how coarsely to
summarise it.

## Your neighbour is worse than nobody

The chart has an odd feature: even the nearest neighbour's adapter is worse than plain Maia-3, by 1.3 to 2 points. A
neighbour carries real information (a random player costs a point or two more), but each adapter also carries its
own noise, trained on 200 games, and at full strength that noise wins.

The fix is to average. Instead of one neighbour, we scored each test game with the average of its 4, 16 or 64
nearest neighbours' adapters.

Averaging 16 neighbours turns a loss of 1.3 to 2 points into a gain: {['700', '1100', '1500', '1900'].map((b) => `+${g(b, 'oracle16_x1.0').toFixed(2)}`).join(', ')}
from 600 to 2000, about 60% of what the player's own adapter gains. Nearby players really do play alike; one adapter
is just a noisy estimate of where they are.

The catch is the caveat in the note. To find a player's neighbours we used their own adapter. For a new player we
tried the obvious stand-in, the players of nearest *rating*, and the average of the whole band. Both are within ±0.07
points of plain Maia-3 in every band: Maia-3 already knows the rating. A new player's neighbours have to be found from
their games.

## Where in Maia-3 the difference lives

The adapter changes four of Maia-3's layers (blocks 5 to 8). Which ones matter? We ran each player's test
game with the adapter switched on in one block only, or in chosen sets of neurons only, and measured how much of the
player's own gain survived. (Switching all of it off gives back Maia-3 exactly.)

**Block 5, the first adapted block, carries the whole effect on its own**, and a bit more:
{B.slice(0, 4).map((b) => ca[b].conds['keep:block5'].retained_lp.toFixed(2)).join(', ')} of the full gain from 600 to
2000. Block 8 keeps about a third. So the later blocks partly undo block 5. (In the 2000+ band the full adapter gains
almost nothing in this test, so its shares can't be read.)

Neurons tell a similar story. Keeping only the 1,024 neurons (of 8,192 in the four blocks) that our attribution ranks
highest keeps {keep('attrib1024').toFixed(2)} of the gain in this band; 1,024 random neurons keep {keep('random1024').toFixed(2)}.
The rankings find the right neurons, at least twice as well as chance at every size we tried, from 16 neurons to 1,024.

What do those neurons compute? We asked language-model agents to write and test small programs that predict
individual neurons from the board. The ones that differ most reliably between players are crisp: *squares the enemy
holds uncontested*, *enemy pieces that are developed and in contact*, *squares our pawns attack*. Those are
candidates for something we want eventually: a style you can name.

## What this means for Doppel

1. **No player types.** Building a bot from "your type" throws information away. Better: place each player in the
   continuous space and borrow from their neighbours.
2. **Borrowing works if you can find the neighbours.** Rating alone can't. The next experiment is to place a new player
   from a handful of their games.
3. **The style is early.** One block's worth of change carries it, which makes cheaper adapters, and more readable
   ones, look possible.
