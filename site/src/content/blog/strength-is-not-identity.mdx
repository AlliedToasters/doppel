A Doppel is meant to play like a particular kind of player, and later like *you*. For that, the model needs a
compact description of a player that it can condition on. Before we train one, we wanted to know what a good
description looks like. So we tested what you can learn about a player from a handful of their games, using
models that already exist.

## The setup

We took 2,000 Lichess players who were not in any training data, with 80 blitz (3+0) games each from October
2026. For each player we built a summary from their first *k* games and asked two questions:

1. **How strong are they?** Predict their rating from the summary (R², 1 is perfect).
2. **Who are they?** Take the summary of their first *k* games and find them among 500 players, each
   summarized by their last 40 games. Chance is 0.2%.

We compared three ways to summarize a game, and all three combined:

- **Clock and opening stats.** Think times, the kinds of moves played, when the player leaves opening theory, and how
  unusual their moves are. No neural network.
- **Maia embedding.** Maia-3 is a neural network trained to predict human moves. Its internal vector for
  each position, averaged over the game, describes how the moves "look".
- **Rating sweep.** We ask Maia-3 how likely each move is for a player rated 800, 1000, … 2400. The rating at
  which the moves look most likely is an estimate of strength.

From one game, the rating sweep already explains 57% of the variance in rating. The embedding needs about five
games to reach that. With 40 games, the combination explains 94%.

Here the picture flips. Even with 40 games, the rating sweep finds the right player only 11% of the time,
because hundreds of players share a strength. The Maia embedding finds them 77% of the time, and the
combination 92%.

## What this means for Doppel

Strength and style are different things, and they need different inputs. The sweep is the best way to read
strength; the embedding carries who the player is. A Doppel's player description should get both: the
sweep for strength, and a model that reads a *set* of games for style.

It also sets a bar. Our model at the time (v0d) found the right player 2.8% of the time at
k = 40. Simply averaging an off-the-shelf embedding does 77%. The next model has to beat that before we can
say it models a player at all.

*Update, 2026-11-13:* it has. On the same 2,000 players and pools, the next version's player description (v0f,
which reads a set of games) finds the right player 97% of the time at k = 40, against 78% for averaged Maia
embeddings and 91% for all three combined. From 10 games it finds 67%, ahead of the combination's 65%.
