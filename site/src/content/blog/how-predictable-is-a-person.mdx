Any model that predicts human moves needs a ceiling: how well *could* it do? People are not deterministic. Ask
the same player the same question twice and you won't always get the same answer. So before judging a bot by
"did it predict the move?", we measured how often a person predicts *themselves*.

## The setup

We took 5.1 million blitz (3+0) Lichess games from October 2026 and looked for a simple thing: the same player
reaching the same position more than once. That happens mostly in the opening, where people play the same lines
again and again. For each position, we asked:

1. **Self-agreement.** When a player is here again, do they play the same move as last time?
2. **Population agreement.** When two *different* players are here, do they play the same move?

Here, "the same position" means the same sequence of moves so far. Reaching the same position by another
move order counts separately. (The version that merges them is in [The position, not the path](/blog/the-position-not-the-path/).)

## People are consistent; populations are not

  title="Agreement on the next move, by move number"

A player who reaches the same position again repeats their move 84% of the time early on, and still 77% by
ply 20. Two different players in the same position agree only 32–58% of the time.

So in these positions, a perfectly *personal* model could predict about 80% of moves, and a perfect model of
"players in general" only about 45%. That 35-point gap is what knowing the person is worth.

## Stronger players are more consistent

At 800, players repeat themselves 75% of the time; at 2400, 92%. Strong players have a prepared repertoire and
stick to it. Weaker players are still exploring, or don't remember what they did last time. So a personal
Doppel of a strong player has an easier job, at least in the opening.

The standard Allie test set shows the same pattern. There, a player's own earlier move predicts 86% of their
repeats at 2400, against 66% at 1000.

## What this means for Doppel

This is our ceiling for opening moves, and our yardstick. A Doppel that models *you* should get close to your own
self-agreement, about 80%, in positions you've played before. A model that only knows your rating does better
than the population number, but not by much: on the positions in the standard test set that a player had
reached before, their own earlier move is right 77% of the time, Maia-3 62% and the most common move among other
players 54%.

The distance between your own 77% and a general model's 62% is the most direct test of whether a bot
knows who it's imitating.

Later positions, the ones nobody reaches twice, have no direct ceiling like this. Repeats fade fast. In 12.7 million
Lichess games from October 2026, 43% of moves at plies 1–9 come in a position the player has been in before. At
plies 10–19 that drops to 3.2%, and at plies 20–29 to 0.15%.
